Hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS) is a serious complication of advanced liver disease characterized by blood vessel dilatation in the lungs, leading to low oxygen levels. While HPS often progresses slowly, it can sometimes accelerate unexpectedly, creating a "critical care window" where rapid intervention is vital. Understanding why this happens and knowing how to act can make a life-saving difference, especially when facing respiratory failure minutes after hospital admission.

What Is HPS?

HPS involves three main features:

Liver disease or portal hypertension

Abnormal dilation of lung blood vessels (pulmonary vasodilation)

Low arterial oxygen (hypoxemia)

These changes impair oxygen exchange. Patients may experience shortness of breath, low oxygen saturation, and fatigue. In most cases, symptoms worsen gradually. However, certain triggers can cause a sudden decline.

Why HPS Can Accelerate Suddenly

Several factors can tip an otherwise stable patient into rapid respiratory compromise:

Infections• Bacterial pneumonia or sepsis can inflame lung tissues and capillaries• Systemic inflammation worsens intrapulmonary shunting

Fluid Shifts• Rapid fluid overload (e.g., aggressive IV fluids) increases lung water• Hepatorenal syndrome can lead to low urine output and fluid retention

Cardiac Stress• Arrhythmias or heart failure reduce blood flow through well-ventilated lung regions• Increased pulmonary pressures promote further vascular dilation

Procedural Complications• Sedation or intubation may depress respiratory drive• Overzealous diuresis can cause electrolyte imbalances and cardiac arrhythmias

When one or more of these factors hit, patients can deteriorate within minutes, sometimes leading to respiratory failure minutes after hospital admission.

The Critical Care Window

The "critical care window" refers to the short period—often under an hour—when aggressive, targeted treatment can reverse or stabilize the decline. Key goals during this window are:

Restoring adequate oxygenation

Reducing pulmonary shunting

Correcting fluid and electrolyte imbalances

Treating underlying triggers (infection, heart dysfunction)

Missing this window can result in multi-organ failure and higher mortality. Rapid assessment, interprofessional teamwork, and close monitoring are essential.

Recognizing Respiratory Failure Minutes After Hospital Admission

Sudden respiratory failure in HPS can be subtle at first. Watch for:

A rapid drop in oxygen saturation despite supplemental oxygen

Sharp increase in work of breathing: flared nostrils, use of accessory muscles

Confusion or agitation from low brain oxygen

Hypotension or rapid heart rate

Laboratory and imaging clues:

Arterial blood gas showing severe hypoxemia (PaO₂ < 60 mm Hg)

Chest X-ray or ultrasound with signs of pulmonary edema or consolidation

Elevated lactate suggesting tissue hypoxia

Being alert to these signs means you can mobilize critical care resources—intensive care unit (ICU) admission, mechanical ventilation, or extracorporeal support—without delay.

What to Do in a Sudden HPS Crisis

Immediate Oxygen Support

High-flow nasal cannula or noninvasive ventilation (e.g., CPAP)

Prepare for early intubation if oxygenation targets aren't met

Hemodynamic Stabilization

Careful IV fluids guided by central venous pressure or ultrasound

Use vasopressors (norepinephrine) if hypotension persists

Treat Underlying Causes

Broad-spectrum antibiotics if infection is suspected

Manage arrhythmias or heart failure per ACLS and cardiology guidance

Correct electrolytes and acid-base disturbances

Monitor Closely

Continuous pulse oximetry, ECG, and blood pressure monitoring

Reassess arterial blood gases every 1–2 hours in acute phase

Consult Specialists Early

Hepatology for liver-related management and transplant evaluation

Pulmonology for ventilator strategies and pulmonary vasodilator options

Critical care team for advanced support (ECMO if indicated)

Prevention and Monitoring

While not all sudden declines are avoidable, proactive strategies can reduce risk:

Routine screening for HPS in patients with advanced liver disease

Avoiding fluid overload: use diuretics judiciously, guided by daily weights and labs

Early mobilization and pulmonary hygiene to prevent infections

Coordination between hepatology, pulmonology, and critical care teams

Speak to a Doctor

HPS and the risk of respiratory failure minutes after hospital admission are serious. Always discuss new or worsening symptoms with a healthcare professional. If you experience severe shortness of breath, chest pain, confusion, or dizziness, seek emergency care immediately. Only a qualified physician can diagnose and manage life-threatening conditions.

Early recognition of sudden HPS acceleration and prompt action within the critical care window can save lives. Stay informed, stay vigilant, and never hesitate to seek expert medical help.
