# Gen Z Guys Want to Quit Watching Porn. These Companies Are Ready to Help.

SETH, A 31-YEAR-OLD water treatment worker in West Texas, has had problems controlling his use of pornography since he first discovered porn at age 11.

“It didn't take long to realize what I was doing wasn't appropriate,” Seth says, “It was just hard to break away.” As he grew older, he found himself thinking about porn all the time. His commitment to his Christian faith led him to work for a church, but his porn habit continued to make his life unmanageable.

“If you're in ministry, you have the expectation to be perfect and engaging in all of this is, by default, what they'll call a moral failure,” he says, “So it's disqualifying on its own; it's harder to seek help.”

First, he tried porn-blocking apps, including faith-based ones, but soon figured out how to get around them. He’d manage a week or so without using porn, but could never make it longer. Last year, Seth saw an online ad for an app called Quittr and downloaded it. With the help of the app, Seth says he has now gone more than six months without using porn.

Seth is not alone in struggling with pornography—and it’s not only religious people who are having problems with it. A study in 2019 found that 11 percent of men (and around 3 percent of women) see themselves as being at least somewhat addicted to porn and find that it has a negative impact on their lives. Problematic use is even more common among young men. One report from the Survey Center on American Life found that 72 percent of young men support making pornography much harder to access online—with Gen Z far more supportive of imposing restrictions compared to guys over 25.

“I see people that, for whatever reason, they are using pornography excessively, and it is ruining their life,” says Josh Grubbs, PhD, director of the Behavioral Addictions and Associated Disorders (BAAAD) Research Lab at the University of New Mexico.

Two new apps, Quittr and Relay, advertise that they can help turn people’s lives around by helping them quit porn.

Abstinence Isn’t Everything

ONE OF THE biggest concerns that experts have about abstinence apps is that online support groups alone don’t have the expertise to treat the severe problems that porn use can sometimes mask.

In Seth’s case, he says that the men in his Quittr group supported him in getting professional help as well. He is currently on an antidepressant and has been in talk therapy to address issues related to the abuse. Since joining Quittr, he’s only had one relapse.

The question of whether men should set a goal of lifetime abstinence from porn—and how they should think about relapse—is another issue. A common approach in addiction treatment, which is often based on the tenets of 12-step groups like Alcoholics Anonymous, is that if you have a single drink or drug even after many years of abstinence, the clock is reset to day one—as if you’d never been in recovery at all.

Research has found that this approach can be demoralizing or even deadly because of what’s called the “abstinence violation effect.” Essentially, when people believe that a single slip sends them back to square one, they tend to binge dangerously, because, "Why not? I’ve already blown it." Then shame can prevent them from returning to their program or seeking further support.

Another question is whether masturbation without porn is acceptable, or if it too should be considered a relapse. It’s here that Relay and Quittr differ most. Relay, which has a clinical advisory board led by certified sex addiction specialists, allows men to set their own goals and determine for themselves what counts as progress—and what counts as abstinence. In contrast, as currently configured, Quittr resets abstinence to zero if someone returns to any porn use, though it is now updating the interface to reflect progress over time, according to Slater.

Rogers says, “We actually don't have a sobriety clock or a timer in the app.” Instead, users define three “circles” of goals to track for themselves, based on ideas from sex addiction therapist Patrick Carnes, PhD.

The innermost circle contains activities that the person wants to cease entirely—for some, this will include any masturbation, with or without porn. The second circle is known as “boundaries.” It contains activities—like masturbation without porn or staying up late doomscrolling—that the person wants to reduce. The outer circle is for self-care activities that users want to increase like working out or eating better or staying connected to friends and family. The app tracks progress in all of the circles over time by percentage, not sequential days.

“Some people are just focused on porn, and masturbation for them is fine,” Rogers says, “We don't really address that intentionally in the curriculum. We help them understand that they can craft their circles to what feels right for them and their values.”

If porn has become a problem for you, Grubbs suggests that the first thing to do is get a clinical evaluation—even if you think porn is the only issue. This will help detect psychological disorders like depression and anxiety that often cause relapse if not addressed. In many years of research and practice, Grubbs says he can only recall one client with a porn problem who had no underlying disorder.

With his clients, Grubbs then works to help them sort out their own values and set realistic expectations. He doesn’t discourage abstinence goals for those who seek them—but provides education on typical sexuality and how to avoid shame associated with relapse.

Although people join apps like Quittr and Relay to address their porn use, the most important thing they seem to get from them is a sense of community and connection. “What we tell people when they come into Relay is, ‘You don't have a porn problem. You have a pain problem,’” says Rogers, “Porn is really just something your brain learned as a coping mechanism, as a strategy, as a solution.”
