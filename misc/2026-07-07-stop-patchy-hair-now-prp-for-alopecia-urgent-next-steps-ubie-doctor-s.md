Why Is Your Hair Patchy?

Patchy hair loss can be unsettling, but understanding the possible causes and treatment options helps you take control. One common reason for round or oval patches of hair loss is alopecia areata, an autoimmune condition where the body's immune system attacks hair follicles. In this guide, we'll explore why hair becomes patchy, how PRP for alopecia areata works, and the medical next steps you can consider.

Common Causes of Patchy Hair Loss

Alopecia AreataAn autoimmune disorder resulting in sudden, well-defined patches of hair loss on the scalp or other body areas.

Tinea Capitis (Scalp Ringworm)A fungal infection causing scaly, itchy bald spots, often in children.

Traction AlopeciaHair loss from constant pulling or tension (tight ponytails, braids).

Telogen EffluviumWidespread shedding after stress, illness, or hormonal changes; usually temporary.

TrichotillomaniaA compulsive urge to pull out your own hair, leading to irregular patches.

Medical Conditions & Nutritional DeficienciesThyroid disorders, lupus, iron deficiency, and other systemic issues can lead to patchy or diffuse hair loss.

Treatment Options for Alopecia Areata

No cure exists yet, but treatments can help regrow hair and control the immune response:

Topical CorticosteroidsCreams or foams applied to patches to reduce inflammation.

Intralesional Corticosteroid InjectionsDirect injections into bald spots; often first-line for limited disease.

Topical ImmunotherapySensitizing agents (e.g., diphencyprone) provoke an allergic reaction to distract the immune system.

Minoxidil (Rogaine)Applied daily to stimulate hair follicles and improve regrowth.

Oral ImmunosuppressantsMethotrexate or cyclosporine for extensive or resistant cases—used under close medical supervision.

JAK InhibitorsNewer oral drugs targeting specific immune pathways; promising but expensive and with potential side effects.

PRP for Alopecia Areata

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) therapy is gaining attention as a safe, minimally invasive option. Here's what you need to know:

What Is PRP?

A concentration of your own platelets enriched with growth factors.

Prepared by drawing a small amount of blood, spinning it in a centrifuge, and isolating the platelet-rich layer.

Injected back into the scalp at targeted sites.

How PRP Helps

Stimulates hair follicle growth by delivering growth factors directly to the scalp.

Modulates inflammation around hair follicles—important in autoimmune conditions.

Enhances cell repair and blood flow, creating a healthier environment for hair.

Evidence for PRP in Alopecia Areata

While most studies focus on androgenetic alopecia, emerging data support PRP for alopecia areata:

A 2017 randomized trial showed significant hair regrowth in mild to moderate alopecia areata patients after monthly PRP injections for three months.

Smaller case series report reduced patch size and new hair growth with minimal side effects.

PRP appears most effective for limited patches (less than 25% scalp involvement).

Safety and Side Effects

Low risk: Uses your own blood, so no allergic reactions.

Mild discomfort: Injections can cause transient pain or scalp tenderness.

No downtime: Most people return to daily activities immediately.

Treatment Protocol

Typically involves 3–6 sessions spaced 4–6 weeks apart.

Maintenance sessions every 3–6 months may prolong results.

Best combined with other treatments, such as topical corticosteroids or minoxidil, for a synergistic effect.

Living with Alopecia Areata

Emotional impact: It's normal to feel self-conscious. Consider support groups or counseling if you're struggling.

Camouflage options: Wigs, scarves, and hair fibers can boost confidence while you wait for regrowth.

Long-term outlook: Some people experience full regrowth, while others see recurrent patches. Keeping open communication with your doctor helps tailor ongoing care.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

Patchy hair loss is rarely life-threatening, but see a doctor right away if you experience:

Sudden hair loss with fever, severe scalp pain, or redness (possible infection).

Rapid, widespread shedding accompanied by weight loss or fatigue (could signal systemic illness).

New neurological symptoms (e.g., muscle weakness, vision changes).
