# What You Need to Know Now About Hydroxychloroquine

THERE ARE SOME medications that become a huge deal fast, only to fizzle out just as quickly. Remember hydroxychloroquine? The drug sparked a lot of controversy at the start of the pandemic, with some people—including then-president Donald Trump—claiming that hydroxychloroquine could lower the risk of getting Covid.

[Need to Know Now](https://innate-signal.jcesqc8.com/blog/1520828494.html)

But the medical community insisted back then that the medication, which is commonly used to treat malaria, wouldn’t keep people from getting Covid. A lot has changed since 2020, including research into effective treatments for the virus. With Trump now the president-elect, things like hydroxychloroquine are bubbling up again.

So, where does hydroxychloroquine fall right now in terms of Covid? Here’s the deal.

What is hydroxychloroquine, again?

HYDROXYCHLOROQUINE IS IN a class of prescription medications called antimalarials, per the U.S. National Library of Medicine. It’s also sold under the name Plaquenil.

[About Hydroxychloroquine](https://peplawllc.com/)

“Hydroxychloroquine works by changing the pH in small compartments in your cells called lysozomes,” explains Jamie Alan, Ph.D., an associate professor of pharmacology and toxicology at Michigan State University. “Lysozomes work to degrade or breakdown proteins in our cells.” Hydroxychloroquine specifically makes those small compartments more acidic, which causes more of a protein breakdown, she says.

What does hydroxychloroquine treat?

HYDROXYCHLOROQUINE IS CONSIDERED an antimalarial and an antirheumatic drug, which gives it a few potential uses.

The biggest use for hydroxychloroquine is to treat or prevent malaria, a serious and potentially deadly parasitic infection spread by mosquitoes, according to the U.S. National Library of Medicine. Hydroxychloroquine is also used to treat some other infections and health conditions, including several forms of lupus and rheumatoid arthritis

“In individuals affected by malaria, hydroxychloroquine acts within red blood cells to kill the malarial parasites that cause the disease,” says Kelly Johnson-Arbor, M.D., a toxicologist at MedStar Health. “Hydroxychloroquine also works on the human immune system to cause anti-inflammatory effects, which is why it is also used to treat lupus and rheumatoid arthritis.”

OK, so why do people think it can prevent Covid?

HERE’S WHERE THINGS get controversial. “In test tube and animal studies, hydroxychloroquine was found to have some antiviral and antibacterial activity,” Dr. Johnson-Arbor says.

Meaning, the idea of using hydroxychloroquine to prevent Covid wasn’t out of left field—and it’s important to remember that there were no treatments for the virus at that time, leaving people scrambling to do what they could to stay safe. “Because it helps regulate the immune system, and also due to its potential antiviral effects, hydroxychloroquine was studied as a potential treatment for Covid-19 infection,” Dr. Johnson-Arbor says.

Unfortunately for those excited about hydroxychloroquine, research on actual humans didn’t pan out.

Can hydroxychloroquine help lower the risk of getting Covid?

NOT ACCORDING TO SCIENCE. Dr. Johnson-Arbor cites a “landmark” study published in The New England Journal of Medicine in 2020 that found that hydroxychloroquine was not an effective treatment for humans hospitalized with Covid infection. Another trial found that hydroxychloroquine was not effective in preventing Covid illness in healthcare workers who were exposed to the virus.

And another study found that hydroxychloroquine wasn’t effective at keeping people who were infected with Covid from being hospitalized with the virus, either.

“Based on the results of these and other studies, at this time there is no evidence that hydroxychloroquine can effectively prevent or treat Covid infection in humans,” Dr. Johnson-Arbor says.

In fact, some early clinical trials on people with Covid who took hydroxychloroquine also found that those patients had a higher risk of dying from the virus compared to those who didn’t take the drug.

“It has not been shown to be effective in humans as a treatment or prophylaxis for Covid,” says infectious disease expert Amesh A. Adalja, M.D., senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security. Something else to keep in mind, according to Alan: “There are significant side effects associated with this drug.”

Those include something called QT prolongation, which is an abnormal heart rhythm that can lead to a fatal heart arrhythmia called Torsades de Pointes. “Other side effects can include increased chance of infection, vision changes, depression and thoughts of self-harm, painful rash, neural problems, depletion of all blood cells, which can be fatal, and muscle aches and pains to name a few,” Alan says. There’s no antidote for taking too much hydroxychloroquine, making this a tricky medication to mess with, Dr. Johnson-Arbor says.

Basically, Dr. Adalja says it’s not a good idea to take hydroxychloroquine if you don’t need it.

Now, there are several potential Covid prevention strategies, including multiple vaccines and Pemgarda, a monoclonal antibody treatment that can be given to people over the age of 12 who are moderately or severely immunocompromised.

So, doctors don’t recommend taking hydroxychloroquine to lower your risk of Covid. “There is harm in taking hydroxychloroquine for Covid-19, and no benefit in Covid-19 according to the available data,” Alan says.
