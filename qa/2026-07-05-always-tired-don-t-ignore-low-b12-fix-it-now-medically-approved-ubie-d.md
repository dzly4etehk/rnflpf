Explanation

Feeling constantly tired—even after a full night's sleep—can be frustrating and confusing. While stress, poor sleep, and busy schedules are common causes, one often-overlooked reason is low b12 vitamin levels.

Vitamin B12 deficiency is more common than many people realize, especially in older adults, vegetarians, vegans, and people with digestive issues. The good news? It's treatable once identified. Below, we'll break down what B12 vitamin does, why levels drop, how to recognize symptoms, and what medically approved steps you can take next.

What Is B12 Vitamin and Why Is It So Important?

B12 vitamin (also called cobalamin) is a water-soluble vitamin your body needs to function properly. It plays a critical role in:

Making red blood cells

Supporting brain and nerve health

Producing DNA

Converting food into energy

Without enough B12 vitamin, your body can't produce healthy red blood cells. This can lead to a type of anemia called megaloblastic anemia, which reduces the oxygen delivered throughout your body—leading to fatigue and other symptoms.

Your body doesn't make B12 on its own. You must get it from food or supplements.

Why Is Your B12 Vitamin Low?

There are several medically recognized reasons why B12 vitamin levels drop.

1. Not Getting Enough from Food

B12 is found naturally in animal-based foods such as:

Meat (especially beef and liver)

Fish and shellfish

Eggs

Dairy products

People who follow vegetarian or vegan diets are at higher risk unless they consume fortified foods or supplements.

2. Poor Absorption

Even if you eat enough B12 vitamin, your body may not absorb it properly.

Common causes include:

Pernicious anemia (an autoimmune condition affecting absorption)

Stomach or intestinal surgery (such as gastric bypass)

Chronic gastritis

Crohn's disease or celiac disease

Long-term use of acid-reducing medications (like proton pump inhibitors)

Metformin (a common diabetes medication)

B12 absorption requires stomach acid and a protein called intrinsic factor. If either is disrupted, levels can drop.

3. Age-Related Changes

As we age, stomach acid production often decreases. Adults over 60 are at higher risk of B12 vitamin deficiency, even with a normal diet.

4. Certain Medical Conditions

Some disorders that affect the digestive system or immune system can interfere with B12 absorption.

Signs and Symptoms of Low B12 Vitamin

B12 deficiency often develops slowly. Symptoms may be subtle at first but can worsen if untreated.

Common Symptoms

Persistent fatigue

Weakness

Shortness of breath

Pale or slightly yellow skin

Dizziness

Headaches

Neurological Symptoms (More Serious)

Numbness or tingling in hands and feet

Difficulty walking or balance problems

Memory problems

Confusion

Mood changes, including depression

Other Possible Symptoms

Sore tongue

Mouth ulcers

Blurred vision

Heart palpitations

Not everyone experiences all symptoms. Some people may have nerve-related symptoms even without anemia.

How Is B12 Vitamin Deficiency Diagnosed?

Diagnosis is straightforward but requires a blood test.

A doctor may check:

Serum B12 levels

Complete blood count (CBC)

Methylmalonic acid (MMA) levels

Homocysteine levels

Sometimes B12 levels can appear "low-normal" but still cause symptoms. Additional testing may help clarify.

If you're experiencing ongoing fatigue, nerve symptoms, or unexplained weakness, it's important to speak to a doctor. Some complications of B12 deficiency can become permanent if left untreated.

What Happens If You Ignore Low B12?

It's important not to panic—but it's also important not to ignore it.

Untreated B12 deficiency can lead to:

Severe anemia

Nerve damage (which may become irreversible)

Balance issues and falls

Increased risk of complications in pregnancy

The earlier it's identified, the easier it is to treat.

Medically Approved Next Steps

If you suspect your B12 vitamin is low, here's what doctors typically recommend:

1. Get Tested

Do not self-diagnose based on symptoms alone. A simple blood test can confirm deficiency.

Ask your doctor about checking:

Serum B12

Complete blood count

Additional markers if needed

2. Address the Underlying Cause

Treatment depends on why your levels are low.

If diet-related → Improve intake or supplement

If absorption-related → Injections or high-dose oral therapy

If medication-related → Review options with your doctor

3. Supplementation

There are two main medically approved treatments:

Oral B12 Supplements

Often effective even in mild absorption problems

Typical doses range from 1,000–2,000 mcg daily (as directed by a physician)

B12 Injections

Used in more severe cases

Often given weekly at first, then monthly

Especially helpful for pernicious anemia

B12 vitamin is generally very safe because excess amounts are excreted in urine.

4. Improve Your Diet

If your deficiency is diet-related, increasing B12-rich foods can help:

Lean meats

5. Monitor Levels

After starting treatment, your doctor will likely recheck levels to ensure improvement.

Symptom improvement can take:

A few weeks for energy levels

Several months for nerve recovery

Longer if deficiency was severe

Are over 60

When to Seek Immediate Medical Care

While most B12 deficiency develops gradually, seek urgent medical attention if you experience:

Severe weakness

Difficulty walking

Sudden confusion

Chest pain

These symptoms may signal serious anemia or another medical condition.

Always speak to a doctor about symptoms that feel severe, sudden, or life-threatening.
