# Stuck in the Same Fights? These 5 Sports Tactics Can Help Solve Your Relationship Stress.

WITH SO MUCH tension in the world right now, the last place you want it showing up is between you and your partner. But conflict and differences are part of every relationship. As a therapist who works with individuals and couples, I’ve seen how easy it is to get stuck in that conflict. When that happens, connection stalls, and it becomes hard to build the kind of supportive, resilient partnership most people want.

[These Sports](https://reboundworks.escortskhi.com/blog/7356370448.html)

The good news is that working through conflict doesn’t have to feel overly clinical or impossible—it can be real, direct and empowering. In fact, some of the most effective strategies are ones that you may have already learned in other areas of your life. As a former Division 1 collegiate athlete, I’ve seen how the mental and team skills used in sports help couples work through conflict. Here are a few of my favorites to put in play—along with how and when they can make the biggest difference in your relationship:

The Strategy: Take one for the team

Helps with: Avoiding resentment

HEALTHY COUPLES often take one for the team. One gets up with the crying baby multiple nights in a row so the other can sleep and be rested for her work presentation. One encourages the other to get out with friends on the golf course even though it means the other has a Sunday alone with three small children. Sacrifice without resentment.

If you find yourself taking a pitch on the body every time you are at bat, though, you may have a bigger issue to address. But if you both generally approach the relationship the same way you would for your team—you both show commitment, effort, and skill, then you can put aside any need to keep score and instead tell yourself in a moment of unequal workload, “I’ll just take this for the team.”

The Strategy: Give Your Teammate an Easy Ball

Helps with: Making a hard truth land

YOUR PARTNER is driving you to the brink, and you know that bringing it up won’t be easy. So set them up for success with an easy ball. In soccer, sometimes you put it to feet, other times to head or to space- you adjust based on what makes it easiest to execute the play. Do the same when delivering a difficult message.

Relationship researchers John and Julie Gottman found that happy couples used a “Gentle Start-Up” when approaching conflict. Instead of blaming, criticizing or attacking, they share concerns calmly, using “I” statements that focus on their hopes, not faults.

Many couples, however, come in too hot. Take Andrew and Alex (whose names are changed here). Their relationship was strong until the pandemic. Andrew’s anxiety spiked— he withdrew, struggled at work, snapped at their son. Eventually, Alex exploded: Marrying you was the worst decision I’ve made. You’re useless—a shell of a partner.” Andrew recoiled in shame, which only made his anxiety worse and deepened the divide.

Instead, Alex needed to communicate the hard truth—that Andrew’s anxiety is pushing their marriage to the edge—in a way that Andrew could actually hear. The easy ball was to start with care and clarity: “I want us to feel close again and for home to feel more connected. I know anxiety hit you hard during Covid. I want to support you. And I also want you to know that it’s a serious issue to me—one that needs to be addressed.”

This approach makes it more likely that Andrew will engage instead of shutting down. It invites him into the conversation, giving him space to process and step up over time.

Difficult truths don’t have to be destructive. When you deliver them with intention—passing the ball in a way your teammate can receive—you do your part in setting the stage for change.

The Strategy: Time your Run

Helps with: Dismantling defensiveness

TIMING MATTERS in conflict. When one partner gets flooded—emotionally overwhelmed—it triggers a fight-or-flight response: the heart races (over 100 BPM, sometimes up to 165), adrenaline pumps, and the brain goes into shutdown mode and defenses go up. In this state, productive conflict is nearly impossible. In fact, John Gottman found that couples in which one partner becomes regularly flooded during conflict are more likely to divorce.

So, when bringing up issues in your relationship, time your run. In soccer, run too early, and you’ll be offside; run too late, you lose your step on the defender. It’s the same in relationships—tune into when and how you bring something up. If either of you is flooded, take a break. Go for a walk, breathe, reset. Then come back to it when you’re both clear-headed and able to engage.

This principle also applies to intimacy. Sometimes, one person wants sex as a way to relieve stress and feel connected, while the other needs to feel connected and relaxed before wanting sex. If you’re the former, lay the groundwork. Have a meaningful conversation the night before. Send a flirty text the morning of. Think of foreplay as a buildup that happens over days, not just minutes before. Set the play up so the timing works for both of you. Be patient, attuned and tactful to set yourself up for success.

The Strategy: Get Yourself Game Ready

Helps with: Mental fitness for conflict

WHEN CONFLICT comes, you’ll want to be game ready. For athletes, preparation is the key to success, and it’s the same for relationships. You need to build emotional and mental fitness for both conflict and connection.

That starts with everyday habits: get attuned to your emotions instead of reacting on impulse. Then, like a great coach, offer yourself the support and strategy you need to move forward.

Here’s what I mean. Take a guy I’ll call Mark. He came to therapy at age 28 spiraling over whether his girlfriend was into him as much as he was into her. Every time anxiety kicked in, he’d cling—texting her incessantly. She’d get annoyed and pull away, which only made him cling harder. The cycle was running him, not the other way around.Mark needed to stop looking for reassurance from his girlfriend and instead look to himself.

So what’s the counter to anxiety?

Take a breath. Don’t just react. Slow things down.

Regulate your mood. Become self-aware and ask yourself what your emotional state is. Be honest and curious with yourself.

Tolerate the feeling. Don’t run from the discomfort. Stay with it and regulate it well. If you’re angry, learn to talk yourself down. If you’re anxious, learn how to reassure yourself.

Strategize. Figure out the best way to solve the conflict. Be fair-minded when assessing the situation. Know yourself and what you really want. Be open to your partner’s input. Viewing the problem from these three angles will allow you to develop a strategy that works.

Mark wanted a deep, meaningful relationship. When he communicated that with confidence—without desperation—he invited her closer. And if she didn’t meet him there? He could break up with her. It wouldn’t be easy, but he could handle it. Like Mark, you can strengthen your inner foundation so that no matter what happens externally, you trust yourself to handle it. Like athletes, you can train to be emotionally steady and mentally strong. When conflict arises, you can then step in with clarity and confidence.

The Strategy: Put the Team Above the Individual

You might have a shot, but you pass it to your teammate who has a better one. In relationships, there are times when you need to shift your mentality to putting the team above the individual.

Have you ever caught yourself telling yourself a negative story about your partner: “Why is she always so caught up with planning?” “Why can’t he ever relax and have fun?” “Are we just too different?” Observe the story that you’re telling yourself. Ask yourself whether that story is reductive and negatively colors the way you hold them in your heart. If so, drop it like it’s hot, not just for your sake, but also because it can ruin the team.

Instead, write a more expansive story that captures each of your perspectives. Write the story from the team’s view, the full arc, not just isolated plays: the grueling practices, working through the differences in playing styles, those heartbreaking losses, the moments when synergy began to form, the hard-fought wins, the fun locker room banter, bus rides home and post-game parties. Successful couples value what relationships can offer. They see its potential and magic. They write the story of the team.
