BACK IN 2016, Manny Almonte joined some friends on a camping trip to Lake George in the Adirondacks of upstate New York. Almonte had grown up in the Dominican Republic, so he’d known nature. But since moving to New York City in the mid-’90s, he hadn’t experienced the powerful restorative benefits bestowed by time in the wild. That camping trip unlocked something for him: Nature was a potent mental health tool—one not tapped by many people.

Almonte would go on to create Camping to Connect in 2018, a nonprofit that runs outdoor trips for young men of color, and soon he was inviting other New Yorkers to spend a few nights under the stars. At first, it was a tough sell.

“I said, ‘Let’s go into nature and go camping,’ and it was like, ‘Hell, nah! That’s what white people do,’ ” Almonte says. Yet people signed up—and reaped the rewards. “It’s nighttime around the fire,” he says, and they’re opening up, “talking about their fathers, and big, fit guys are breaking down and crying.”

I can relate. I’ve spent a lifetime outdoors and experienced this kind of social bonding, deep conversation, and real emotion. I’m so drawn to the power of the outdoors that I’ve shaped my life around it. Where I live (a small town in the southern Rockies), who I spend time with, how I work and play—I’ve optimized my access to the wild. Only recently, though, have I begun to understand exactly how and why the environment contributes in a big way to overall health and happiness.

Some of that insight comes from a book by Marc G. Berman, PhD, associate professor of psychology at the University of Chicago, called Nature and the Mind: The Science of How Nature Improves Cognitive, Physical, and Social Well-Being. For Berman, nature’s power has everything to do with attention, the driving force behind our thoughts and behavior. And if you just checked your phone while reading that last sentence, you know that screens are our attention’s greatest enemy.

Berman says that our lack of access to nature (or unwillingness to go out in it) has contributed to soaring rates of stress, chronic disease, loneliness, and other mental health issues. Reconnecting to nature can be part of the antidote. Berman, who dubbed his field “environmental neuroscience,” argues that coming into contact with nature on pretty much any scale—from a sprawling national park to a local garden to a greened-up indoor space—can redirect and restore our often frazzled and overwrought minds.

The mechanism behind this nature gap is explained in something called attention restoration theory, an idea that was developed in 1992 by Stephen and Rachel Kaplan, who were psychology professors at the University of Michigan. The theory divides attention into two types. The first is directed attention, which we use for work, sports, doomscrolling. The second type is involuntary attention, which engages when we’re ambling in the woods. Directed attention, Berman explains, is fatigable. Involuntary attention, not so much. “A lot of these natural environments sit right at this nexus of being softly fascinating,” he says.

Parks, forests, shorelines—they all have the unique ability to elicit soft fascination, which gives our brains a break. Except that plenty of us don’t live near those places. Since around 2007, more than half of the world’s population has migrated to urban areas—a milestone in human history. (By 2050, experts speculate, about 70 percent of us will be urbanites.) Plus, access to the outdoors tends to break along socioeconomic fault lines.

The good news is that we don’t need to move to a desert island where Amazon doesn’t deliver in order to reap some of nature’s rewards. Indoor environments can be “naturized,” says Berman. When plants, pictures, and architectural elements that resemble patterns found in nature are added to our surroundings, they can increase attention, reflective thoughts, and feelings of restorativeness. Even synthetic plants and landscape images can provide a mental boost. Studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging revealed that “functional network connectivity at rest was higher when participants were seeing natural rather than built environmental photographs,” according to results published in the journal Nature.

Exactly how much nature, and what kind, our brains need isn’t quite clear yet, says Berman. Spending time outdoors in a natural setting is optimal, and if you have the means, aim to apply the 20-5-3 rule developed by Rachel Hopman-Droste, PhD, a researcher at Pearson. She recommends spending 20 minutes in green space three times a week, 5 hours in semi-wild environs once a month, and 3 days off-grid once a year as a minimal effective dose.

If that’s too lofty a goal, try to optimize your place into a green space. One study found that people felt better mentally in rooms that had plants. They also showed improved blood pressure numbers and signs of enhanced cognition.

When I mention the growing research on nature and well-being to Manny Almonte of Camping to Connect, he tells me about the many times he’s brought inner-city kids to the woods on their first overnight trip. “We collect their phones—something they’re told about before they go—and you can see the pain on their faces,” he says. “But that doesn’t compare to when we ask them to take off their shoes and put their bare feet on the grass, a grounding exercise. They’re like, ‘What? Ouch!’ But when they do it, when they get comfortable with it, you see their whole demeanor change. You give them permission to feel the grass, and it’s like a revelation.”

Meet Some Men Going All In On Nature-Maxxing

If you need a little inspiration, read below on some notable figures who have managed to balance their busy lives without forsaking nature time.

Martin Heinrich, 53

US Senator, D-New Mexico

[Neuroscientists Say Follow](https://satietybrief.china-akan.com/blog/3548283742.html)

When Heinrich was elected senator in 2012 and moved to Washington, D.C., he decorated his new city office with artifacts and images of wild places. “I have European mounts, landscape paintings, Navajo rugs, and a window with views of trees and sky,” says the sportsman and public lands advocate. “I really don’t feel like myself when I don’t get enough time outside. I really worry that too many kids don’t have that kind of on-ramp to the outdoors. Every kid growing up in this country should be within 10 minutes’ walking time of an outdoor space.”

Rick Tallman, 62

Founder, Tallman Pacific Consulting Firm

Tallman spent 20 years in Denver, working “24/7” while running as many as three sustainable-energy companies at once. For balance, he’d unplug with a weeklong solo backpacking trip. “I needed to be in a city for work, and I was doing a lot of travel,” he says. “I realized, if nature is going to be part of my life, I have to do it in this relatively short but immersive way. It really does stick with you. I used to joke that one week in nature by myself helps me tolerate the human race for the other 51 weeks.”

Thosh Collins, 43

Indigenous Educator, Trainer

Collins trains outside every chance he gets, both for the diverse workout challenges he can find in deploying functional movement—lifting rocks or logs in what he calls “Earth gym”—and for the quiet moments of reflection and gratitude. “You’re on the land and taking a mindful moment. You’re not thinking about what happened yesterday or what’s going on in social media. You’re putting these things aside for a moment and reconnecting to your humanness and to what matters—to what provided life.”
