Why Your Skin Feels Like It's Burning: The Science of Nerve Inflammation

A burning sensation in your skin can be alarming. Whether it flares up after a hot shower, follows an insect bite, or appears for no clear reason, that intense sting often signals nerve inflammation. Understanding why your skin feels like it's burning helps you recognize potential causes, seek appropriate care, and find relief.

What Is Nerve Inflammation?

Nerve inflammation (neuritis) occurs when peripheral nerves—those outside your brain and spinal cord—become irritated or damaged. Unlike a simple heat or chemical burn, this irritation tricks your pain receptors (nociceptors) into firing constantly, creating a persistent burning feeling.

Key players in this process include:

C-fibers and Aδ-fibers: Small nerve fibers that carry pain and temperature signals to your brain.

Inflammatory mediators: Chemicals like cytokines and prostaglandins released by your immune system activate receptors on nerve endings.

TRPV1 receptors: Also called capsaicin receptors, these detect heat and inflammatory signals, amplifying the burning sensation.

When these components become overactive, even a light touch or normal body temperature can feel like your skin is on fire.

Common Causes of a Burning Sensation

Nerve inflammation can arise from a variety of conditions. Some of the most frequent include:

Diabetes (diabetic neuropathy)

Shingles (postherpetic neuralgia)

Small fiber neuropathy (often idiopathic or autoimmune)

Compression neuropathies (e.g., carpal tunnel syndrome)

Nutrient deficiencies (vitamin B12, vitamin B6)

Chronic alcohol use

Chemotherapy-induced neuropathy

Autoimmune disorders (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis)

Infections (Lyme disease, HIV)

Trauma or surgery affecting nerves

Symptoms You Might Experience

Burning skin often comes with other sensory changes. Pay attention to:

Tingling or "pins and needles"

Stinging, shooting pains

Numbness or loss of sensation

Heightened sensitivity (allodynia), where light touch hurts

Redness, warmth, or swelling in the affected area

Muscle weakness (if larger nerves are involved)

These symptoms can be constant or intermittent, and may worsen at night.

How Doctors Diagnose Nerve Inflammation

Accurate diagnosis starts with a detailed history and physical exam. Your doctor will ask about:

Onset and pattern of your burning sensation

Associated symptoms (weakness, rash, systemic signs)

Medical history (diabetes, autoimmune disease, alcohol use)

Medications and recent illnesses

Diagnostic tests may include:

Blood tests: Check blood sugar, vitamin levels, thyroid function, autoimmune markers.

Nerve conduction studies (NCS): Measure electrical signals in large nerves.

Electromyography (EMG): Assesses nerve-to-muscle transmission.

Skin biopsy: Evaluates small nerve fiber density for small fiber neuropathy.

Imaging: MRI or ultrasound if nerve compression or structural issues are suspected.

Treatment Options

Managing a burning sensation involves tackling the underlying cause and soothing nerve irritation. Treatments may include:

Anticonvulsants: gabapentin, pregabalin

SNRIs or TCAs: duloxetine, amitriptyline

Topical agents: capsaicin cream, lidocaine patches

Anti-inflammatories: NSAIDs or short-course steroids

Nerve blocks or injections

Self-Care and Lifestyle Tips

While under a doctor's care, you can also:

Soak in cool (not cold) baths with colloidal oatmeal

Wear loose, breathable clothing to reduce friction

Practice gentle stretching and low-impact exercise

Use stress-reduction techniques (meditation, deep breathing)

Keep your skin moisturized to prevent cracking and infection

These measures soothe irritated nerves and support overall nerve health.

When to Seek Medical Help

A burning skin sensation is usually manageable, but certain red flags warrant prompt attention:

Rapidly spreading or severe burning pain

New muscle weakness or loss of coordination

Fever, chills, or signs of systemic infection

Rash accompanied by intense burning (possible shingles)

Unintentional weight loss or night sweats

If any of these occur, don't wait—seek medical evaluation.
